# Implications — what to actually do with this

Three audiences, in order of relevance: **anyone choosing what to build**, **anyone choosing a
career**, and **this repo's actual business** (restaurants), because the restaurant sits at the
intersection of four of the twelve families and is the single best natural laboratory available here.

---

## Part 1 — The five selection rules

Distilled from the taxonomy and stress-tested against [`04-counterarguments.md`](04-counterarguments.md).
If you remember nothing else:

### Rule 1 — Human contact is not a moat. Human contact *plus a supply constraint* is.
Care work is the most human work there is and is paid terribly, because supply is large and
replenishable. The value accrues to whoever holds the scarce asset: the license, the venue, the
credential, the reputation, the relationship. **Ask of any opportunity: what stops the tenth person
from doing this next year?** If the answer is "nothing, it's just hard and human," you have a job,
not a business.

### Rule 2 — Prefer categories whose demand *scales with* automation.
The AI ombudsman, escalation tiers, identity recovery, liability underwriting, robot wrangling,
in-person verification — these get bigger as AI gets better. They are structurally hedged. Compare
with categories that depend on AI *not* being good enough, which are short-volatility bets on
capability plateauing. It won't.

### Rule 3 — Sell to employers and payers, not to lonely individuals.
The affordability ceiling is the binding constraint on almost every consumer version of these
categories. The same product sold to a company (retention, health cost, compliance) or a payer
(avoided hospitalization) supports 10x the price and doesn't depend on the customer being solvent.
Corporate offsites are a $73.7B industry; consumer "community" businesses mostly aren't businesses.

### Rule 4 — Look for what the internet killed that only worked because forgery was hard.
In-person proctoring came back. The reference check is coming back. Notarization, physical
originals, witnessed signatures, wet ink, in-person closings. This is a systematically under-searched
category because it looks backward. Every one of these was killed by a digital shortcut, and every
one of those shortcuts is now cheaply defeatable.

### Rule 5 — Bundle across families.
Single-family businesses commoditize. The durable positions sit in three or more:
hospitality (presence + belonging + taste + ritual), the contractor (embodiment + liability +
verification), the personal physician (touch + signature + transformation + continuity). Bundling is
the moat.

## Part 2 — Careers

The test, applied to any role:

> **Can the output be delivered as a file?** If yes, it deflates. If it requires a body, a signature,
> a witness, or a relationship that took years to build, it inflates.

Four practical notes:

**On the bridge jobs.** Teleoperation, expert data supply, tacit-knowledge capture — these pay
extraordinarily well right now (up to ~$200/hr for credentialed judgment on expert marketplaces) and
they are explicitly funding their own obsolescence. Treat them as a **capital accumulation phase**,
not a career. Take the money, build the asset that outlasts it.

**On credentials.** They're bifurcating. Credentials that signal *knowledge* are deflating fast,
because anyone can now produce knowledge-shaped output. Credentials that confer *the right to be
accountable* — licensure, bar admission, PE stamp, medical license, fiduciary status — are
appreciating, because they're permission to bear liability. **Choose credentials for the liability
they let you carry, not the knowledge they certify.**

**On the trades.** Genuinely booming, genuinely well-paid, and worth one caution: the enrollment
surge is partly demographic (a bigger cohort), not purely a preference shift. And a decade out,
structured physical environments automate before unstructured ones. The durable position is
*trade + ownership + accountability* — the licensed contractor who carries the risk — not the pair
of hands.

**On the professions.** Watch your own industry's billing model. **The transition from billing time
to billing risk is the tell that a profession has completed the shift to Family 4.** Whoever moves
first inside a firm captures the leverage; whoever moves last competes with machines on hourly cost
and loses.

## Part 3 — The restaurant case (the natural experiment in this repo)

This is not a detour. The restaurant is the single most instructive worked example of the whole
thesis, and this repo happens to own one ([Wok & Karahi](../../flagship/)) plus a consulting
business selling to hundreds more.

### Why restaurants are a four-family business

| Family | What the restaurant sells |
|---|---|
| **1 — Presence** | A room where something happens once, at a specific table, on a specific night |
| **5 — Belonging** | Regulars. Being known. The third place, in its original and most literal form |
| **7 — Taste** | Somebody decided what you should eat, and you trusted them |
| **8 — Witness** | Birthdays, anniversaries, deals, first dates, wakes. Restaurants are where transitions get marked |

Danny Meyer's version of this is instructive: doubling down on human-to-human interaction, including
a staffed in-house "experience curator" reservations team whose job is to craft the occasion before
the guest arrives ([USHG](https://www.ushg.com/danny-meyer)). That is a deliberate bet that the
*human* layer is the product and the food is the medium.

### The strategic split, and it's stark

Restaurants are being pulled apart into two businesses that used to be one:

- **The delivery/logistics business** — a commodity throughput operation. Fully exposed to
  automation, aggregators and price competition. Deflating.
- **The hospitality business** — presence, belonging, taste, witness. Inflating, and the *only*
  place with pricing power.

Most independents are currently defending the deflating half (fighting over commission points) while
under-investing in the inflating half. That's backwards, and it's a positioning argument AZ can
make credibly because it runs a restaurant.

### Six concrete plays that fall out of the taxonomy

1. **Sell occasions, not covers.** Family 8. The restaurant is already where people mark
   transitions; almost none of them capture it deliberately. Knowing that table 6 is a 40th
   anniversary and acting on it is a witness product, not a service upgrade — and it's the highest
   margin thing on the floor.
2. **Regulars as the actual asset.** Family 5. Recognition is the product AI can't deliver and
   aggregators actively destroy. A guest who is *known* is a guest with no price sensitivity and no
   reason to comparison-shop.
3. **Chef's counter / small-room premium.** Family 1's small-room inversion. Per-head economics of
   intimacy beat scale. This is the omakase pattern arriving in every cuisine, and it arrives in
   dining before it arrives in music.
4. **Human on the phone as a premium signal — deliberately.** This one cuts *against* part of what
   this repo sells, and the honest version is a split: automate the throughput calls (hours,
   directions, order status) and make sure a real person answers the ones that carry an occasion or
   a complaint. If human contact is becoming the luxury tier across the whole economy, a restaurant
   that answers with a person on the calls that matter is buying a premium signal cheaply. **The
   error would be automating the calls where being known is the product.**
5. **Local human recommendation.** Family 7's local variant. As AI recommendations homogenize
   globally, the neighbor-who-knows becomes the differentiated discovery channel — and it is
   defensible in a way that SEO no longer is.
6. **Phone-free or otherwise scarcity-engineered service nights.** Family 1, T3. Low cost, high
   differentiation, strong press hook, and it directly rides the fastest-growing event trend of 2026.

### The uncomfortable one

Restaurant technology consulting is itself partly a Family 4/11 business — **the human who is
accountable for the tech stack, and who fights the vendors on the operator's behalf.** That framing
is more defensible than "we set up tools," because tool setup is exactly the kind of work that
deflates. Worth revisiting the positioning docs in [`../`](../) with this lens later.

## Part 4 — If this becomes a build

Ranked by conviction, from [`03-frontier-weak-signals.md`](03-frontier-weak-signals.md):

1. **AI ombudsman / redress** — demand scales with automation, no incumbent, real consumer pain,
   plausible B2B2C wrapper via employers or insurers.
2. **In-person verification as compliance** — enterprise budget, fraud-driven urgency, regulatory
   tailwind. Boring, which is why it's available.
3. **Care coordination sold to employers** — clear buyer (retention of the sandwich generation),
   enormous unpaid labor pool to convert.
4. **Escalation-tier-as-a-service** — arming SMBs with a credible human premium tier, which is
   exactly the customer base this repo already reaches.
5. **Identity/account recovery** — acute, underserved, currently handled by luck.

The pattern across all five: **not a business that resists AI, but a business that exists because of
it.**
