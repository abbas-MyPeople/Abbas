# The Human Experience Economy

**Side project.** Research into a single proposition: *every profession involving human-to-human
contact or human experience is about to go bananas.*

Started **2026-07-28**. Internal research — not customer-facing, robots-disallowed like the rest of
[`../research/`](../).

---

## The short version

The popular framing — "AI can't do human stuff, so human stuff wins" — is already falsified. 72% of
US teenagers use AI for companionship. What actually holds up is narrower and stranger, and it comes
in two halves.

**The defensive half.** AI collapses the price of cognition and of infinitely-reproducible output.
Everything whose value came from being *hard to produce* deflates. Everything whose value comes from
being *impossible to reproduce* — a body in a room, a person who can be sued, a night that happened
once, a hand on a shoulder — inflates against it.

**The offensive half, and the more useful one.** AI does not disintermediate. It **re-intermediates**.
It kills the middleman who moved *information* and promotes the middleman who moves *trust*. When
supply becomes infinite, **the interface becomes the product — and the highest-converting interface
is a specific person somebody trusts.** That's a Whatnot host doing $8B in live GMV, a creator
putting 300 covers through a restaurant with one video, a forward-deployed engineer clearing $1.2M
because model capability is abundant and context isn't.

Three corollaries worth holding onto:

- **It's mostly a story about prices and status, not mass employment.** Where it *is* about
  employment (care work), it's about badly-paid employment — the largest job growth in the US
  economy is also near-minimum wage.
- **The durable categories are boring.** Accountability, verification, advocacy, redress — not the
  concerts and supper clubs everyone cites.
- **The biggest risk isn't AI doing human contact better.** It's AI doing it *cheaply enough*, so
  the people who most need connection can only afford the synthetic version.

## The five things worth remembering

1. **The recording/concert split.** In every industry, ask which half is the recording (reproducible,
   deflating toward zero) and which is the concert (unreproducible, inflating). Recorded music went
   to $0.003 a stream; live went to $25.2B for one company. *Same songs.* Only the reproducibility
   changed. Education, medicine, law and restaurants are all splitting the same way right now.

2. **Human contact is not a moat. Human contact plus a supply constraint is.** Care work is the most
   human work there is and it's paid terribly, because supply is large and replenishable. Ask what
   stops the tenth person doing this next year.

3. **SPLIT is the modal outcome, not BOOM.** About a third of the 280 categories bimodalize — the
   transactional middle hollows out while a premium tier and a low-cost tier both survive. The
   dominant career risk isn't replacement, it's **being stranded in the middle**.

4. **Prefer categories whose demand scales *with* automation.** The AI ombudsman, escalation tiers,
   identity recovery, liability underwriting, forward-deployed anything. These get bigger as AI gets
   better, rather than betting on capability plateauing.

5. **Scarcity is the product; humanity is just its current form.** None of this is really about
   humans being special. It's about scarcity migrating. The durable strategy isn't "be human" — it's
   *own something that cannot be reproduced*, and right now a lot of those things happen to be human.

## Method, and its known limits

Web research across ~30 searches, July 2026, spanning labor statistics, live events, care, trades,
verification, robotics, agentic commerce, live commerce, religion, ritual, analog reversion and the
creator economy. Sources are graded A–D in [`02`](02-evidence-ledger.md) and low-grade claims carry
their qualifier wherever they're used.

**Two limits stated up front:**

- **Confidence grading is right for accuracy and wrong for discovery.** Institutional sources only
  describe categories that already have names, which is a lagging indicator by construction. The
  correction is Engine 4 in [`09`](09-interface-thesis-and-generative-engines.md) — deliberately
  hunting where money moves *before* the label exists.
- **The original method was defensive.** It asked what humans can do that AI can't, which finds
  refuges and misses positions. Document 09 diagnoses this properly; documents 00–08 should be read
  knowing it.

**Open items:** eight claims still need primary sources — listed in [`02` §10](02-evidence-ledger.md).
Top of the list is the originating quote itself, attributed in conversation to "Mark Anderson" and
plausibly either Marc Andreessen (a16z) or Mark R. Anderson (SNS/Future in Review). Nothing here
depends on it, but it deserves proper attribution.

## Where this touches the day job

Restaurants sit in **four families at once** — presence, belonging, taste and witness — which makes
them one of the best natural experiments available for this thesis, and this repo happens to own
one. Two findings carry directly into positioning:

- **The restaurant is splitting into two businesses.** Delivery/logistics is the deflating half;
  hospitality — the room, the regulars, the occasion — is the inflating half with all the pricing
  power. Most independents are defending the wrong half.
- **AZ Restaurant Partners is structurally a forward-deployed engineering firm** — an engineer who
  owns a restaurant, physically shows up, and translates general technology into one operator's
  specific mess. That's the highest-paid role structure in technology right now, described in
  restaurant language. See [`09` §Engine 5](09-interface-thesis-and-generative-engines.md) and
  [`05` Part 3](05-implications-and-plays.md).
