# Where this thesis breaks

A thesis this congenial to believe needs an adversary. Everything in this folder is the kind of idea
that feels good — humans matter, presence wins, the machines can't take the things that count. That
is exactly the profile of an idea that gets adopted on vibes and then quietly fails.

Below: the seven strongest arguments against, ranked by how much damage each does. Then the parts of
the thesis that survive all of them.

---

## 1. "Goes bananas" hides four different outcomes, and three of them aren't good for you

**The argument.** The thesis conflates *price boom*, *volume boom*, *status boom*, and *format boom*.
They are not the same thing and they rarely co-occur.

The clearest case is care work. Home health aides are the single largest source of new US jobs
through 2034 — +739,800, more than any other occupation. That's a volume boom of historic size, in
the most irreducibly human work there is. **And it is one of the lowest-paid occupations in the
country.** Volume booms in an occupation with a large, replenishable labor supply produce *more
poor people doing that job*, not prosperity.

Meanwhile live music is booming in revenue while employing roughly the same number of artists —
that's a price boom captured by venue owners, ticketing platforms and headliners, not a labor story
at all.

**Damage: severe.** This is the most important correction in the document. "Human contact work
booms" is compatible with "people who do human contact work stay poor." Baumol says the *sector*
gets more expensive; it says nothing about who captures the surplus. Historically, the answer is
whoever owns the scarce asset — the venue, the license, the brand, the platform — not the person
doing the touching.

**What survives.** The thesis holds for work that combines human contact with a *supply constraint*:
licensure, credential, reputation, physical scarcity, or ownership. Human contact alone is not a
moat. **Human contact plus a barrier to entry is.**

## 2. AI companionship may be a substitute, not a complement

**The argument.** The whole thesis assumes AI can't satisfy the demand for human connection. The
evidence increasingly says it partially can, and that partial satisfaction may be enough to
suppress demand for the real thing.

72% of US teenagers have used AI for companionship. Character.AI has ~20M MAU, more than half under
24. AI companion apps grew ~700% between 2022 and mid-2025. And the research points at
**deskilling**: reliance on AI companions may transform relational norms in ways that make
human-human connection feel *less* accessible and *less* rewarding
([APA](https://www.apa.org/monitor/2026/01-02/trends-digital-ai-relationships-emotional-connection),
[Drexel](https://drexel.edu/news/archive/2026/April/teen-AI-chatbot-addiction)).

This is worse than simple substitution. Simple substitution would be a market split. Deskilling is a
*ratchet*: the more you use the frictionless option, the less capable you become of tolerating the
friction of real people, and the less you demand it. Human contact is a taste that has to be
maintained.

**Damage: severe, and this is the live one.** Both trends are growing simultaneously right now —
run clubs *and* AI companions — which means we genuinely cannot yet tell whether they're serving
different populations, different needs, or the same need in a way that will eventually resolve to
one winner.

**What survives.** The categories where the human is required for a *reason other than emotional
satisfaction* — accountability, verification, physical touch, witness, embodiment. Those are immune
to this argument. Family 5 (Belonging) and parts of Family 6 (Transformation) are not.

**Test to run.** Longitudinal in-person social time among heavy AI-companion users vs matched
controls. If it falls, Family 5 is in real trouble.

## 3. The affordability ceiling — human contact becomes a luxury good, which makes it a small market

**The argument.** If human service prices as premium (force F7), then by construction most people
can't buy it. The consumer data is not supportive: the K-shaped economy persists into 2026, the Fed's
May 2026 Beige Book has high-income households resilient and low-income households strained, and
spending growth is concentrated in the top two quintiles who own ~80% of stocks. CNBC's "E-shaped"
framing has the middle class "spending in a nervous way."

Live music makes the point concretely: revenue records are being set while ticket prices are up
~25% since 2019 and VIP packages run four figures. That's not broad-based demand — it's demand
concentrating among people who can absorb price increases.

Combine with §1: **human contact for the affluent, AI contact for everyone else.** A real market,
but a top-two-quintile one, and a socially corrosive outcome that shouldn't be described as
"bananas" without saying what it costs.

**Damage: high.** It doesn't falsify the thesis; it shrinks it and changes its moral valence.

**What survives.** Categories where a third party pays — employers (offsites, benefits, care),
payers/insurers (care coordination, behavioral health, loneliness contracting), or the state
(education, elder care). **The B2B and B2Payer versions of every category in the taxonomy are more
durable than the consumer version.** That is probably the single most actionable line in this
document.

## 4. Robots close the embodiment gap faster than expected

**The argument.** Families 2, 3 and 10 (Touch, Hands on the World, Care) rest on the claim that
physical work is hard to automate. That's a statement about the *current* data bottleneck, not a law
of nature. The binding constraint today is manipulation data: a skilled teleoperator produces 5–50
episodes/hour, and quality degrades with fatigue.

Every serious lab is attacking exactly that constraint — egocentric human video, robot-free
demonstration collection, sim-to-real, cross-embodiment transfer. If any of those work, the
constraint dissolves quickly, because unlike human labor, robot capability replicates at software
speed once learned.

Note the recursive trap: **Family 12 (Human Experience as Input) is being paid, right now, to
destroy Families 2, 3 and 10.** Teleoperators are well-compensated specifically for generating the
data that removes the need for teleoperators.

**Damage: moderate near-term, severe long-term.** Probably not before 2032 for unstructured
environments; plausibly much sooner for structured ones (warehouse, kitchen line, standardized
construction).

**What survives.** Physical work bundled with *judgment, liability or relationship*: the electrician
who is also the accountable contractor, the nurse who is also the clinical decision-maker, the aide
the family trusts. Pure dexterity is the most perishable asset in this entire folder.

## 5. The authenticity premium is stated preference, not revealed preference

**The argument.** 68% say they'd choose human-made over identical AI-made **at the same price** —
which measures preference, not premium, and says nothing about behavior at a 40% discount. Survey
respondents reliably overstate willingness to pay for virtue attributes; the fair-trade and organic
literatures are full of this gap.

The most telling data point cuts against the enthusiasts: Capgemini found an explicit "human-made"
label adds almost nothing over no label — because in 2026 people still *assume* human by default.
The premium is currently invisible because it's not yet contested.

And the AI-backlash figures — 36% "punished a brand" — come from a single vendor press release with
unpublished method. That's D-grade evidence carrying a lot of narrative weight.

**Damage: moderate.** It doesn't touch the structural families (accountability, verification,
embodiment) at all. It hits Family 7 (Taste) and Family 8's commercial edge hardest.

**What survives.** Two things. First, the *category* where human-made is inseparable from the product
(live performance, craft, hospitality) — nobody buys a concert ticket for the label. Second, the
prediction that **the label's value switches on when the default assumption flips.** That's a
testable, dated prediction rather than a vibe: watch for the first market where consumers assume AI
unless told otherwise.

## 6. Regulation removes humans instead of requiring them

**The argument.** Force F3 (the liability sink) is a political fact, not a physical one. The current
direction — EU AI Act Art. 14, NIST RMF, 700+ US bills — favors mandated human oversight. But
precedent runs both ways: autonomous vehicle regulation has moved steadily toward *removing* the
required human, and did so faster than most predicted once safety data crossed a threshold.

The moment automated decisions are demonstrably safer than human ones in a domain, mandating human
review becomes politically indefensible — it starts reading as protectionism that kills people.
Radiology and diagnostic pathology are candidates for exactly that argument within a decade.

**Damage: moderate.** It's slow — professional licensure is among the stickiest institutional
structures we have, defended by well-organized incumbents. But it is not permanent.

**What survives.** Accountability is a deeper need than regulation. Even where the law stops
requiring a human signature, *tort* and *insurance* still need a defendant. Someone must be sued.
Family 4 relocates from licensure to liability, which is a harder thing to legislate away.

## 7. It's a wealth-effect story that reverses in a downturn

**The argument.** Much of the experience-economy boom is discretionary spending funded by asset
appreciation in the top quintiles. Experiences are the *most* deferrable category of consumption
there is — you can skip a concert far more easily than rent, food or insurance.

If the AI capex cycle corrects, the same wealth effects unwind, and the experience economy contracts
hard and fast. The 2008 analogue is not encouraging for restaurants, travel or live events.

**Damage: moderate, but it's a timing risk rather than a thesis risk.**

**What survives.** The non-discretionary families: care (F6 demographics don't care about the
business cycle), accountability/verification (compliance spend is countercyclical if anything), and
trades (infrastructure has long lead times). The discretionary families — presence, belonging,
transformation, taste — are the cyclical ones. **A portfolio built only on the exciting half of this
taxonomy is a leveraged bet on asset prices.**

## What survives all seven

Strip out everything the counterarguments damage, and the residue is small, unglamorous, and much
more defensible than the headline thesis:

1. **Accountability work** (Family 4) — someone must be suable. Survives everything except a legal
   revolution, and even then relocates to insurance.
2. **Adversarial verification** (Family 9) — where the opponent is another intelligence trying to
   deceive you, there is no stable automated equilibrium.
3. **Advocacy and redress** (Family 11) — demand scales *with* automation volume, so it is hedged
   against the very thing that threatens everything else.
4. **Care** (Family 10) — demographics are arithmetic. But expect volume without prosperity unless
   labor organizes or immigration policy tightens supply.
5. **Physical work bundled with judgment** (Family 3 ∩ Family 4) — the contractor, not the pair of
   hands.
6. **Presence with genuine supply constraint** (Family 1, the owned-asset version) — the venue, the
   room, the license. Not the performer.

Note what's *not* on that list: most of the fun stuff. Belonging, taste, transformation, the human
premium — all real, all growing, all substantially more fragile than they look.

---

## The honest summary

The thesis is **directionally right and popularly misunderstood**.

Right: relative prices are shifting toward things that require a body, a signature, a witness or a
relationship. Every one of the nine forces is real and several are independently sufficient.

Misunderstood in three specific ways:

- It is mostly a story about **prices and status**, not about mass employment. Where it *is* about
  employment (care), it's about badly-paid employment.
- The most durable categories are the **boring, adversarial, liability-shaped** ones — not the
  concerts and supper clubs everyone cites.
- The largest single risk isn't that AI does human contact better. It's that **AI does human contact
  cheaply enough, and the people who most need connection can only afford the cheap version.**

That last one is the thing to keep watching. It's the difference between a thesis about opportunity
and a thesis about stratification, and right now the evidence supports both.
