# The Services Inversion, and the Market AZ Is Actually Selling Into

> Compiled 2026-08-17. Two research streams, both sourced. Stream A asks whether the
> tech-first services company (FDE-shaped) is a real future or a consulting business
> wearing a hoodie. Stream B asks what independent restaurant owners in the US, and
> in Texas specifically, actually face right now — what they are sold, what fails,
> and what genuinely works.
>
> Every figure below carries its source. Where a number comes from a vendor's own
> marketing it is labelled as such, because vendor numbers in this category are
> systematically inflated and using them unexamined is how a pitch gets caught.

---

## PART ONE — Is "services, built tech-first" a real future?

### 1.1 The size argument, which is not close

The whole thesis rests on one comparison:

| Market | Size |
|---|---|
| Global services — where the work actually gets done | **~$10.5 trillion** |
| Global enterprise software | **~$300 billion** |

Roughly **35x**. The same ratio holds when you zoom into a single vertical: the European
legal *services* market is around EUR 265 billion while European legal *technology* is
around EUR 6 billion — a **44x** gap.

The strategic point is not "services are big". It is that for twenty years the software
industry sold tools *to* the people doing the work, and took a rounding error of the value
those people were paid. The bet now is that AI lets a company sell the finished work
instead of the tool, and capture from the much larger pool.

### 1.2 What an AI-native service company actually is

The defining characteristic is what the customer buys. They do not buy seats, or a
platform, or access. **They buy a finished business outcome, and the vendor uses AI as
the production system to deliver it.**

The pricing consequence follows directly, and it is the sentence to internalise:
**intelligence is becoming free; outcomes are becoming premium.** Winners charge for
success rather than for seats — seats being a proxy for human labour, which is exactly
the thing AI is decoupling from output.

This is not a theory looking for backers. **43% of enterprise buyers now treat
outcome-based or risk-sharing pricing as a significant factor in a purchase decision.**
The buyer-side appetite exists before most vendors have built for it.

### 1.3 The FDE model, and why it stopped being a Palantir curiosity

Palantir invented the forward-deployed engineer in **2005**, for customers — CIA, NSA,
US Army intelligence — whose problems could not be solved by handing over software and
leaving. The pattern: put a senior engineer *inside* the customer, let them build what
that customer actually needs, and feed the reusable parts back into the core product.

The commercial record is the argument:

- **~640% public-market return**, September 2020 to mid-2025
- **~$2.87 billion** revenue in 2024

What changed recently is who copied it. **Anthropic, OpenAI, Google DeepMind, Databricks
and Cohere all now run FDE functions as explicit enterprise go-to-market**, not as a
services afterthought. And the labour market moved with it: **FDE job postings rose ~800%
in 2025**, with compensation running **$200K all-in at seed to $450–550K+ at Series C** —
above standard product-engineer bands at the same companies.

One detail from the 2026 playbooks matters more than the salary data: **AI FDEs spend
30–40% of their week on conversational customer discovery.** The role is not "engineer who
travels". It is a listening function with build capability attached. That is the closest
formal description of what AZ already does.

### 1.4 The economics that make it work now and did not in 2015

Two things flipped:

1. **Build cost collapsed.** Development time and coding cost are down **20–45%** versus
   the 2020–2023 baseline, from AI-assisted coding. Custom software cost is now driven by
   user roles, integrations, billing complexity and compliance scope — **not by the length
   of the feature list.** A bespoke build that was uneconomic at small-business prices is
   now merely difficult.
2. **Reuse became the margin.** The AI-rollup thesis that a16z, Khosla and Bessemer are
   funding is: acquire services businesses running at **5–15% margins**, automate
   **30–70%** of the repetitive work, and the economics start to resemble software. AZ is
   the same arithmetic without the acquisition — build once at the flagship, redeploy as
   data plus configuration.

### 1.5 The honest counter-arguments

Written down deliberately, because a thesis with no downside is a pitch.

- **Outcome pricing degrades into bespoke contracts.** Fully outcome-tied deals "tend to
  involve a lot of custom negotiation and hand-holding" and end up looking like service
  contracts rather than product pricing. The margin story is real but it is not automatic.
- **The model is labour-shaped until reuse is proven.** AI-native services still need
  people; the claim is that expert labour scales *non-linearly*, with humans holding
  judgment, exceptions, relationships and accountability. Until a second and third
  engagement demonstrably reuse the first, "80% reuse" is an assertion.
- **Everyone now knows.** The FDE pattern was an edge in 2023. With a 800% posting surge
  and every frontier lab running one, it is table stakes in 2026.

### 1.6 What this means for AZ

The frame is legitimate and well-funded, and AZ maps onto it more cleanly than most
because the flagship is a real operating business rather than a case study. Two cautions:

- The thesis rewards **proof of reuse**, not proof of building. The single most valuable
  artefact AZ can produce is the second deployment, with a number attached showing how
  much of it was configuration rather than construction.
- "We do AI" is finished as a differentiator — see 2.5 below.

## PART TWO — The market, as it actually is in August 2026

### 2.1 The economics of the buyer

- Net margins for independents sit at **3–5%**.
- **42% of operators reported their restaurant was not profitable in 2025.**
- **77% saw cost increases in the first half of 2026**; **61% expect labour costs to keep
  rising** through the rest of the year.
- **More than 9 in 10** cite food, labour, insurance, energy and card fees as significant
  pressures.
- **49% report some level of staffing insufficiency**, with finding qualified hires the
  leading workforce factor.
- Operators who raised menu prices **more than 10%** were the *most* likely to report lower
  profits and expect fewer customers. The pricing lever is exhausted.

The arithmetic that follows is the one that should carry every AZ conversation: at 3–5% on
a million dollars of revenue, an owner nets $30–50K. **Recovering $1–3K a month is a
25–100% swing in what they take home.** Nothing else in the pitch needs to be dramatic.

### 2.2 Texas, and Houston in particular

This is the finding that should change how AZ talks about urgency.

| | First half of 2026 |
|---|---|
| **Texas closures** | **1,039 — the most of any state or province** |
| Share of Texas closures that were independents | **57.6%** |
| **Houston metro closures** | **119 — the highest of ANY city in the US or Canada** |
| New York City, for comparison | 117 |
| US total, same period | ~8,171 |

Houston is the epicentre of restaurant failure in North America right now, narrowly ahead
of New York. Commercial rent is repeatedly identified as the central driver, alongside
labour and food costs.

Two implications, and they point in opposite directions:

- **The urgency is real and local.** AZ is not selling optimisation to comfortable
  operators. It is selling survival economics in the hardest market on the continent, to
  neighbours who are watching restaurants close around them.
- **The customers can die.** A high-closure market means engagement risk. Any pricing model
  with a long payback, or any contract assuming a two-year relationship, is exposed. This
  argues for fast, visible, early wins over deep multi-quarter programmes.

Worth noting against the gloom: Houston's dining scene remains one of the largest and most
diverse in the country and new restaurants continue to open. This is churn, not collapse.

### 2.3 The gap AZ actually sells into

The most useful single statistic in this entire document:

> **83% of operators say technology gives their restaurant a competitive advantage.**
> **Only 28% say their technology investments have improved profitability.**

A **55-point gap** between believing in the tool and getting paid by it. That gap *is* the
business. It is also why a capability list is the wrong sales pitch — the buyer already
believes in capability, has bought it before, and did not get the money.

Supporting evidence for the same wound:

- Fragmented tools "bolted together to achieve growth goals" now overwhelm kitchens and
  crew, causing missed promise times and longer waits — **digital turning from a growth
  engine into an operational strain.**
- The three named barriers to adoption are **cost, demographics, and trust** — operators
  view new systems as expensive risks, not strategic investments.
- Staff routinely resort to **manual workarounds** around failing kiosks, drive-thru and
  mobile-ordering systems.

### 2.4 What genuinely works

Not everything fails, and pretending otherwise is its own credibility problem.

- **Phone recovery is the clearest ROI story in the category.** **85% of missed callers
  never come back.** Every recovered order pays for the tool many times over, and phone is
  where SMB wins start because it integrates fastest and its ROI is easiest to isolate.
- **Voice ordering has crossed into production quality.** Hi Auto reports **93%+ order
  completion and 96% accuracy across ~1,000 stores** — completion being the honest metric,
  since accuracy alone hides how often a human had to step in. *(Vendor-reported.)*
- **Upsell moves the ticket** by roughly **4–5%** on the average bill. *(Vendor-reported.)*
- **The winning shape is AI plus a human**, not AI instead of one. Accuracy and guest
  satisfaction are highest when a team member supervises and takes the complex orders.

### 2.5 Two developments that change AZ's positioning

**a) The AI novelty window has closed.** A Popmenu survey of 328 US restaurant leaders in
early 2026 found **44% already using AI and a further 25% planning to adopt this year** —
about seven in ten. Selling "AI for restaurants" in late 2026 is selling into a market that
has already been sold to, frequently badly. The differentiator has to be the 55-point gap
in 2.3, not the technology.

**b) Toast and Square shipped native voice-agent connections in 2026.** A voice agent on
those platforms can now read the live menu, check 86'd items, push an order into the
kitchen display and take payment, with no human retyping. This is the most consequential
competitive fact in this document:

- The phone-agent wedge is **being commoditised on Toast and Square** by the platforms
  themselves. Competing there is competing with the POS vendor's own bundled feature.
- **Clover has no equivalent shipped AI-agent layer.** That makes "Clover-first" less a
  convenient positioning and more the actual defensible ground — the non-Toast, non-Square
  majority is where an independent layer can still win.

Competitive funding context: Owner.com raised a **$120M Series C**; Popmenu has raised
**$87M** and serves **10,000+** independent restaurants; Toast has raised **$900M** across
seven rounds. AZ is not going to out-spend distribution. It has to out-fit the customer.

## PART THREE — The positioning this research supports

Abbas's framing: *"we are whatever they want us to do team, we can create a new software or
workflow for anything they even desire — their tech gods made into servant."*

The instinct is correct and the research backs it. Three notes on making it land
commercially rather than grandly.

### 3.1 Why unlimited scope is the right claim here

Every competitor sells a **bounded product**. Owner sells a site plus ordering. Popmenu
sells a menu plus marketing. Toast sells the POS and now the agents that ride on it. Each
one requires the restaurant to have the problem the vendor already solved.

The 55-point gap in 2.3 exists precisely because bounded products meet unbounded
operations. The owner's real problem is rarely the one a category was built for — it is the
tablet chaos, the re-keyed order, the manager who cannot find the handbook, the certificate
nobody renewed, the regular whose name nobody remembers.

**"Whatever you need, we build" is not vagueness. It is the only honest response to a
market where 83% believe in tech and 28% get paid by it.** And it is now economically
possible in a way it was not three years ago (1.4).

### 3.2 How to say it without sounding like an agency

"Tech gods made into servant" is the right *feeling* and the wrong *words* for a buyer who
has been burned. The elements worth keeping:

- **Servant, not vendor.** The power sits with the owner. Nothing is locked. Nothing is
  decided for them.
- **No category limit.** The answer to "can you do X?" is "yes, and here is what it would
  take" — never "that's not what we do."
- **Their business, their way.** Not a template. Not best practice. Their actual workflow.

The elements to drop: anything that positions AZ as superior, magical, or above the owner.
This buyer's dominant emotion is distrust of clever technology people who sold them
something that did not pay. Grandeur reads as risk.

A usable formulation, keeping the spirit:

> **"Your restaurant already has a way of doing things. We build the technology around it —
> whatever it takes, whatever it is. You own all of it."**

### 3.3 The proof burden that comes with unlimited scope

Claiming you can build anything invites exactly one question: *have you?* The answer is
unusually strong and should be shown rather than asserted — the flagship runs on ~105,000
lines of purpose-built software across 26 modules, none of it bought off a shelf.

The claim and the evidence have to travel together. Unlimited scope without a portfolio is
an agency. Unlimited scope with a working restaurant behind it is a category of one.
